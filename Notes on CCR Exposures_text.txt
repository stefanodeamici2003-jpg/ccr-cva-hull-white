2 Counterparty Credit Risk (CCR)
Counterparty Credit Risk (CCR) is the risk that one of the parties involved in a finan-
cial contract will be unable to meet its obligations before the maturity of the contract,
thereby causing a loss to the other party.
This risk is primarily associated withover-the-counter (OTC) markets, where
contracts are privately negotiated and exposure is borne directly by the counterpar-
ties. By contrast, CCR is far less relevant forexchange-traded derivatives, which
rely on standardized contracts, daily mark-to-market procedures, margin requirements,
and when there is the interposition of acentral counterparty (CCP), all of which
significantly mitigate counterparty risk.
2
---PAGE 3---
CCR is typical of contracts with deferred cash flows and uncertain values that de-
pends on market conditions, such asderivatives(interest rate swaps, currency swaps,
options, commodity swaps...) traded OTC. At inception, these contracts usually have
a value close zero or very low, but it evolves over time. The risk materializes when a
contract has a positive value for one party and the counterparty defaults.
CCR differs fromtraditional credit riskin several aspect.
Regarding traditional credit risk:
•it is typical ofloans and bonds;
•exposureknown ex ante;
•exposures evolves in a relativelypredictablemanner;
•it is aunilateralrelationship: the creditor is exposed to the debtor and not vice
versa;
•it arises at the moment theprincipal is disbursed.
By contrast, counterparty credit risk:
•is typical ofderivatives and financial contracts
•the exposure isnot known at inception;
•exposure istime-varyingas it depends onmarket factors;
•it is abilateralrelationship: each party may alternate between creditor and
debtor;
•it may emergelong after contract inception.
Historically, modern Counterparty Credit Risk emerged with the development of con-
temporary financial markets. While counterparty risk was minimal before the twenti-
eth century due to simple instruments and immediate settlement, it became significant
from the 1970s onward after the collapse of the Bretton Woods system increased market
volatility. The rapid expansion of OTC derivatives, initially for hedging and later as a
core banking activity, brought CCR to the forefront, though it long remained underes-
timated. Its systemic nature was fully revealed during the 2007–2008 global financial
crisis, particularly following the failure of Lehman Brothers, leading to post-crisis re-
forms such as Basel III, the introduction of CVA capital charges, greater use of CCPs,
and stricter collateral requirements to enhance financial stability.

Counterparty risk can bemitigatedthrough contracts and more complex mechanisms,
but it can never be fully eliminated. Risk mitigants always leave residual counterparty
risk, which is often harder to measure than the original risk. Importantly, mitigants
usuallytransformcounterparty risk into other types of financial risk rather than re-
moving it altogether (e.g. liquidity risk, legal risk, operational risk, or market risk).
Break Clauses,NettingandCollateralcan be considered in this framework as risk
mitigant.
2.2 Break Clauses
Break clauses are contractual provisions that allow one (unilateral) or both (bilateral)
parties to terminate or restructure a financial transaction either mandatorily, option-
ally, or upon the occurrence of a defined event, most commonly a deterioration in credit
quality such as a rating downgrade.
They are widely used in derivatives and other long-dated financial contracts as tools
to manage counterparty credit risk.
5
---PAGE 6---
Historically, before the global financial crisis, break clauses were mainly demanded by
banks when trading with weaker or uncollateralised counterparties. After the GFC,
concerns about bank creditworthiness led asset managers, pension funds, and other
clients to increasingly require break clauses linked to banks’ own credit ratings.
Mandatory breaks are sometimes used to bypassinternal credit limits, often with
an informal expectation that the trade will be re-executed at the break date.
Exercising a break clause does not always lead to immediate termination. Alternatives
may include providing a replacement counterparty, and in case of termination, the
tradeis usually settled at its prevailing mark-to-market (MTM) value, often determined
via dealer quotations, which is generally simpler and less contentious than close-out
valuation used in default scenarios. From a risk and regulatory perspective, break
clauses differ significantly in effectiveness:
•Counterparty risk reduction: all break clauses are viewed as beneficial,
though optional and rating-based breaks are less reliable because their exercise
is discretionary or dependent on lagging indicators.
•Liquidity coverage ratio risk: rating-based triggers are particularly costly
because banks must pre-fund potential liquidity needs arising from them.
•Capital relief: it is relief is generally achievable only for mandatory breaks.
Optional breaks rarely qualify unless they are exercised consistently, while Basel
rules explicitly disallow capital relief from rating-based triggers.
On the other hand, break clauses come at a cost:
•Moral hazard: sales teams may push for deal execution by relying on the
presence of a break clause to obtain internal credit approval and to offer longer
maturities to clients. However, once the trade is in place, sales may oppose
the exercise of the break from the bank’s side in order to preserve the client
relationship, resulting in increased xVA position and higher RWA consumption;
•Reliance on credit rating: they are typically slow-moving indicators of credit
quality, with downgrades often occurring only once a counterparty’s financial
distress has already become severe.
•Cliff-edge effects: the widespread use of similar rating-based triggers across
counterparties can generate cliff-edge effects, whereby relatively small events,
simultaneously activate break clauses, collateral calls, or termination rights. This
can lead to sudden and system-wide liquidity stress.
•Model risk: break clauses are hard to model and hedge. Rating transition
probabilities cannot be inferred from market prices and rely on limited historical
data, making valuation uncertain. Mandatory breaks are the main exception, as
they can be modeled deterministically.

2.3 Netting
Aclose-out netting agreement, such as the ISDA Master Agreement, provides the
legal framework that allows assets and liabilities arising from derivative transactions
(of any maturity, whether in- or out-of-the-money) to be netted and collapsed so that
only a single net amount is owed when a counterparty defaults.
If the surviving party owes money, then it makes this payment; if it is owed money,
then it makes a bankruptcy claim for that amount.
Underthislegalagreementbetweentwocounterparties, derivativetradescanbegrouped
into anetting set, which represents the level at which counterparty risk and related
risk metricsare calculated.
Two counterparties may have multiple netting sets, for example, to reflect different
jurisdictions, legal entities, asset classes, or regulatory requirements. In some cases,
individual trades may also be treated as separate netting sets.
In rare cases where netting is not permitted or its enforceability is uncertain, each
trade is treated as a standalone netting set, resulting in higher counterparty exposure.
Consider two parties,Bank BandCounterparty C, trading under an ISDA Master
Agreement that includes a valid close-out netting clause.
From perspective of partyBtowards counterpartyCthe set of trades in place is com-
posed by:
•Interest rate swap:+10(Bis owed 10 byC);
•FX forward:−6(Bowes 6 toC);
•Equity option:+4(Bis owed 4 byC).
Without netting:
•Gross amount owed toB:10 + 4 = 14;
•Gross amount owed byB:6.
Multiple obligations remain outstanding between the two counterparties.
With netting, all positions are offset:
Net exposure= 10−6 + 4 = 8.
Therefore only a single net amount of8is owed byCtoB.
The benefits of a netting agreement are:
•a reduction in exposures and, therefore, in counterparty credit risk;
•lower collateral posting requirements;
•a reduction in the number of cash flows exchanged;
•a simplification of the close-out process in the event of default;

•lower regulatory capital requirements, in terms of exposure at default (EAD) and
risk-weighted assets (RWA).
On the other hand, netting may create or amplify the following risks:
•Legal risk: in the presence of different jurisdictions or inadequate legal frame-
works, close-out netting may not be enforceable and the expected benefits may
fail to materialize;
•Systemic risk: netting increases interconnectedness among market participants,
so the default of a single counterparty can transmit losses and liquidity stress
across the financial system, amplifying contagion during periods of crisis;
•Concentration risk: by aggregating many transactions into one net exposure,
netting concentrates risk toward a small number of counterparties, making losses
larger and more severe if one of them fails.

2.4 Collateral
The ISDA Master Agreement may be supplemented by a Credit Support Annex (CSA),
which governs the exchange of collateral.
Collateralconsists of cash or assets set aside, like money kept "in a safe", and is ex-
changed between counterparties as a safeguard to cover exposures and reduce potential
losses in the event of default.
By limiting unsecured exposures arising from changes in the mark-to-market value of
contracts, collateral mitigates counterparty credit risk and supports overall market sta-
bility within modern regulatory frameworks.
In doing so, it converts counterparty risk into:
•Liquidity risk: the ability to source sufficient collateral, particularly during
periods of market stress;
•Market risk: fluctuations in the value of posted collateral and the presence of
wrong-way risk, especially if collateral is non-cash asset;
•Operational risk: risks related to margining processes, valuation disputes, and
settlement failures.
TheCredit Support Annex(CSA) is the legal document governing the collateral-
ization process. It defines the eligible collateral and sets the rules for their valuation
and exchange.
The CSA specifies:
•eligible assetsthat may be posted as collateral, such as cash or specified secu-
rities, usually government, supranational or investment-grade bonds,

---PAGE 9---
•haircuts, a percentage discount applied to the market value of the collateral in
order to account for price, liquidity, and foreign exchange (FX) risks, when the
posted assets are non-cash or denominated in a different currency.
Then, the total collateral is composed of two componentsIndependent Amount(IA)
andVariation Margin(VM):
•independent amount(IA), is a portion of collateral always posted indepen-
dently of the current mark-to-market of the netting set. It is typically fixed over
time and is returned only upon termination of the transaction, provided that
no default has occurred. Initial margin is intended to provide extra protection
buffer between two margin calls and to cover potential losses and costs arising
during the close-out period following a default. Historically, IA has been uncom-
mon in bilateral OTC derivative transactions, while the almost equivalent initial
margin (IM) has long been standard practice for exchange-traded derivatives and
transactions cleared through central counterparties (CCPs).
•variation margin(VM), reflects changes in the mark-to-marketM tMvalue
of the positions and is adjusted dynamically over time. It serves to cover the
variabilityoftheportfoliooftransactionsinquestionandhaslongbeenacommon
feature of OTC derivative collateral arrangements.
The VM exchange conditions are governed by CSA parameters such as:
•amargining frequency, most commonly daily between large institutions -
though weekly and monthly frequencies are standard - which determines how
often exposures are recalculated and collateral is adjusted, through amargin
call, the formal request to post additional or return excess collateral;
•athreshold, representing the level of exposure that may remain unsecured. If
M tM < T hreshold, there is no need to post VM, while ifM tM > T hreshold,
only the excess exposure above the threshold is subject to a margin call;
•aminimum transfer amount(MTA), which is the minimum amount below
which no margin call is triggered, to prevent operationally inefficient small trans-
fers, in particular of non-cash margin;
•acapwhich may limit the maximum amount of collateral that can be demanded,
balancing risk mitigation with liquidity management and operational efficiency.
In the CSA other details are defined, such as:
•thevaluation agent,valuation dateandvaluation time,i.e.the agent
responsible for the evaluation ofM tMon a specific date and time, to avoid
litigation;
•rounding rulesto apply to the margin call amount to simplify the transfers;
•thecut-off timeandsettlement period, the time of the day before which
the margin call must be notified and the period of time allowed to transfer the
collateral requested;
